How to Calculate Tile for a Floor or Wall

Calculating tile quantity is straightforward once you know the formula. The key steps are: measure the area to be tiled, determine the area each tile covers, divide to find the base tile count, then add your waste factor to get the final order quantity.

The Tile Calculation Formula

The basic formula for calculating tiles is:

  • Room Area (sq ft) = Room Length × Room Width
  • Tile Area (sq ft) = (Tile Width in inches × Tile Height in inches) ÷ 144
  • Base Tile Count = Room Area ÷ Tile Area (rounded up)
  • Total Tiles to Order = Base Count × (1 + Waste % ÷ 100) (rounded up)

For example, a 10×12 room (120 sq ft) using 12×12 inch tiles (1 sq ft each) needs 120 tiles as a base. With a 10% waste factor, you'd order 132 tiles — or 11 boxes of 12.

Why the Waste Factor Matters

No matter how skilled the installer, cutting tile to fit room edges, corners, doorways, and obstacles always produces offcuts. Damaged or cracked tiles during installation are also common, especially with larger formats that are more brittle to handle. The waste factor ensures you have enough material to complete the job without a mid-project trip to the store — and more importantly, without the risk of purchasing from a different dye lot that won't match.

Standard waste factor guidelines:

  • 10% — Simple rectangular room, straight lay pattern, experienced installer
  • 15% — L-shaped or irregular room, or diagonal (45°) installation pattern
  • 20% — Complex layout, herringbone pattern, first-time installer, or large format tiles

Tile Size Considerations

Tile size significantly affects both the look of the finished floor and the complexity of installation. Smaller tiles (4×4, 6×6) can follow irregular surfaces more easily and have more grout lines, which can improve slip resistance. Larger tiles (12×24, 24×24) create a more modern, seamless look but require a flatter subfloor and more precision during installation.

Large format tiles (anything over 15 inches in any dimension) are classified as such by the TCNA (Tile Council of North America) and require:

  • Subfloor flatness within 1/8" over 10 feet (vs. 3/16" for standard tiles)
  • Medium-bed mortar or large-format mortar to avoid hollow spots
  • Back-buttering tiles for better adhesion coverage
  • Proper grout joint sizing — typically 1/16" to 1/8" minimum

Diagonal vs. Straight Patterns

The installation pattern is the single biggest driver of waste after room size. A straight or grid pattern (tiles parallel to walls) is the most efficient — cuts at room edges are typically large usable pieces. A diagonal pattern (tiles rotated 45°) runs tiles on an angle across the room. Every piece along the wall perimeter must be cut at 45°, and those triangular offcuts are generally too small to reuse. Expect 15–20% waste for diagonal layouts.

Subfloor Preparation Matters

Before calculating material, assess the substrate. Tiles crack when installed on a flexible or uneven surface. For floors, the subfloor must be dry, flat, and structurally sound. Concrete must pass a moisture test. Wood subfloors typically need cement board or uncoupling membrane (like Schluter Ditra) as an intermediate layer. Skipping proper subfloor prep is the most common cause of tile installation failures — and it means ripping out and replacing all your material.
