How to Size an Air Conditioner

Choosing the correct AC tonnage is one of the most important decisions in any cooling system installation. The right size keeps your home comfortable, controls humidity, and minimizes energy costs. The wrong size — whether too large or too small — leads to comfort problems and shortened equipment life.

What Is AC Tonnage?

1 ton of cooling = 12,000 BTU per hour. The term "ton" dates to the era of ice-based cooling, when one ton of ice would absorb 288,000 BTUs as it melted over 24 hours — equal to 12,000 BTU/hr. Modern residential central air conditioners range from 1.5 tons (18,000 BTU/hr) up to 5 tons (60,000 BTU/hr), sold in half-ton increments.

Tonnage by Climate Zone

  • Mild climate — Plan for approximately 1 ton per 550–650 sq ft (0.0025 tons/sq ft baseline).
  • Moderate climate — The standard US baseline: roughly 1 ton per 400–500 sq ft.
  • Hot climate — Desert Southwest, Southern US: 1 ton per 330–400 sq ft (0.003 tons/sq ft).
  • Hot and humid — Gulf Coast, Florida, Deep South: 1 ton per 300–370 sq ft (0.0033 tons/sq ft). Humidity dramatically increases the effective cooling load.

Insulation's Impact on AC Size

Insulation quality significantly affects how much heat enters or leaves your home. A poorly insulated home in any climate will require 20% more cooling capacity than the same home with good insulation. High-performance insulation reduces system requirements and dramatically lowers operating costs over the equipment's lifetime.

SEER Ratings and Efficiency

Once you know the correct tonnage, also consider the SEER (Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio) rating of the equipment. Higher SEER ratings mean lower operating costs. The federal minimum is 14 SEER in most regions (15 SEER in the Southeast and Southwest as of 2023). SEER ratings of 18–25 are available for premium efficiency units. A higher-SEER unit of the correct size is almost always a better investment than a lower-SEER unit, especially in hot climates where the AC runs frequently.

Common AC Sizing Mistakes

  • Using "one size up" for comfort — Oversizing causes short-cycling, poor humidity control, and early compressor failure.
  • Ignoring humidity — In humid climates, latent cooling load (removing moisture from the air) can be as important as sensible cooling load (lowering air temperature). Undersized units struggle; oversized units never run long enough to dehumidify.
  • Not accounting for sun exposure — South- and west-facing rooms with large windows can have dramatically higher cooling loads than the overall square footage suggests.
  • Replacing with the same size — If the existing system was the wrong size, installing the same tonnage in a replacement perpetuates the problem. Always recalculate the load.
