Concrete Estimation: What Every Contractor Should Know

Accurate concrete estimation is one of the most important skills in construction. Order too little and the pour is interrupted — a critical problem since concrete begins setting within 90 minutes. Order too much and you're paying for wasted material, disposal fees, and the driver's waiting time. Professional estimators always calculate carefully and add a 5–10% overage buffer.

Understanding Cubic Yards

Concrete is ordered and priced by the cubic yard (often called a "yard" on job sites). One cubic yard equals 27 cubic feet. Ready-mix concrete trucks typically hold 8–10 cubic yards, and most suppliers have a minimum order — often 1 cubic yard — with short-load fees for smaller quantities. For projects under about 1 cubic yard, pre-mixed bags are more cost-effective.

The basic formula for any rectangular slab is: Cubic Yards = (Length × Width × Thickness in feet) ÷ 27. Always convert thickness to feet before calculating — for example, a 4-inch slab is 4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 feet thick.

Pre-Mix Bag Types: 60 lb vs 80 lb

Pre-mixed concrete bags are available in several sizes. The two most common for residential projects are:

  • 60 lb bags — Each bag yields approximately 0.45 cubic feet of concrete. These are easier for one person to handle and mix, especially when working by hand.
  • 80 lb bags — Each bag yields approximately 0.60 cubic feet of concrete. Fewer bags are needed for the same volume, reducing cost and trips to the store, but the added weight requires more effort to lift and mix.
  • 90 lb bags — Available from some manufacturers, yielding approximately 0.675 cubic feet per bag.
  • 40 lb bags — Smaller bags for repairs and small patches, yielding about 0.30 cubic feet each.

For large projects exceeding 1–2 cubic yards, ordering ready-mix delivered by truck is almost always more economical and ensures a consistent mix quality.

Concrete PSI Ratings

Concrete strength is measured in PSI (pounds per square inch) — the compressive strength after 28 days of curing. Choosing the right PSI for your application is critical:

  • 2,500 PSI — Minimum residential grade; suitable for non-structural applications and light-duty pads.
  • 3,000 PSI — Standard residential grade; appropriate for driveways, sidewalks, patios, and garage floors.
  • 3,500 PSI — Heavy-duty residential and light commercial; used for driveways subject to heavy vehicles, thick slabs, and most footing applications.
  • 4,000 PSI — Commercial and structural applications; used for structural footings, retaining walls, and high-traffic commercial floors.
  • 5,000+ PSI — High-strength structural concrete for commercial construction, bridges, and engineered applications.

Standard Concrete Slab Thicknesses

Different applications call for different slab thicknesses. Using too thin a slab leads to cracking and structural failure, while an overly thick slab wastes material unnecessarily:

  • Sidewalks and walkways — 4 inches thick, 3,000 PSI minimum
  • Residential patios — 3.5–4 inches thick, 3,000 PSI minimum
  • Residential driveways — 4 inches thick for passenger cars; 5–6 inches for heavy vehicles
  • Garage floors — 4–6 inches thick, 3,500–4,000 PSI recommended
  • Structural footings — Varies by load; typically 6–12 inches, as specified by an engineer

Reinforcement: Rebar vs Fiber Mesh

Unreinforced concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension — it cracks under bending loads. Two common reinforcement methods are:

  • Rebar (Reinforcing Bar) — Steel bars placed in a grid pattern within the slab. Standard residential slabs use #3 rebar (3/8" diameter) on 18-inch centers. Rebar provides excellent structural strength and is required for most footings and structural applications.
  • Fiber Mesh — Polypropylene or steel fibers mixed directly into the concrete. Fiber mesh helps control plastic shrinkage cracking and is easier to use than rebar for simple residential slabs. It does not replace rebar for structural applications.
  • Wire Mesh — Welded wire fabric placed mid-slab. Common in residential driveways and slabs; less effective than rebar but easier to install.

Concrete Ordering Tips

When ordering ready-mix concrete, communicate clearly with the batch plant:

  • Specify the PSI strength required (e.g., 3,000 PSI, 28-day)
  • Specify the slump — the wetness/workability of the mix (4-inch slump is standard for most residential work; wetter mixes are easier to work but weaker)
  • Specify any additives such as air entrainment (for freeze-thaw climates), accelerators, or retarders
  • Always add 5–10% overage to your calculated volume to account for waste, subbase irregularities, and spillage
  • Confirm the truck access to your site — concrete trucks weigh 60,000–70,000 lbs when loaded and can damage driveways and landscaping
